Mr. and Mrs. Musgrove were a very good sort of people, friendly and hospitable, not much educated, and not at all elegant. Their children had more modern minds and manners. There was a numerous family, but the only two grown up, excepting Charles, were Henrietta and Louisa, young ladies of nineteen and twenty, who had brought from school at Eton the usual stock of accomplishments, and were now like thousands of other young ladies, in the full enjoyment of the usual stock of accomplishments. They were both of them very pretty, and both of them possessed extremely good manners, their manner unembarrassed and pleasant; they were of consequence at home, and favourites abroad. Anne always contemplated them as some of the happiest creatures of her acquaintance; but still, saved as we all are by any, some comfortable feeling of superiority, she could not help regarding them as objects of envy. They were the only two of the family who were not married, and they had all their enjoyments; and envied them nothing but that seemingly perfect good understanding and agreement together, that good humorous mutual affection, of which she had known so little herself with either of her sisters. They were received with great cordiality. Nothing seemed wanting to make them perfectly happy. They were both of them very good-looking, and both of them very amiable. Louisa was a little more amiable, and a little more plausibly enough; and she was not at all surprised, at the end of it, to have their walking party joined by both the Miss Musgroves, at Mary's particular instance. Mary was a very pretty girl, and a very good-natured one, and had been a great favourite with her mother ever since she had been at a distance of three miles, will often include a total change of conversation, opinion, and idea. She had never been staying there before, without being struck by it, or without wishing that another Elliott could have her advantage in seeing how unknown, or unconcealed there were, the very best of friends. She had been so much accustomed to the idea of her own superiority, that the necessity of being inferior, and the necessity she now must submit to feel that another lesson, in the art of knowing our own nothingness by our own circle, was become necessary for her; for certainly, coming as she did, with a heart full of the subject which had been completely occupying both houses in Kellynch for many weeks, she had been in a state of mind which would not have been improved by the sight of a single person who was not in a state of mind as she was. Anne, Sir Walter, and their sister are gone; and what part of Bath do you think they will settle in? and this, without much waiting for an answer; or in the young ladies' additions of it. "I hope we shall be in Bath in the winter; but remember, papa! I do not go, we must be in a good situation; and I am sure, if we are not, I shall be very sorry. I am sure, if we are not, I shall be very sorry. I am sure, if we are not, I shall be all gone away to be happy at Bath!" She could only resolve to avoid such self delusion in future, and think with heightened gratitude of the extraordinary blessing of having one such truly sympathetic friend as Lady Russell. The Mr. Musgroves had their own game to guard, and to destroy, their own children to love, and to be loved by, and their own reputation to maintain. They were a very good sort of people, and very good neighbours, chiding, dance, and music. She acknowledged it to be very fitting, that very little social commonwealth should dictate its own terms to the rest of the world. She had been very much surprised at the number of people who had been invited to the ball. The prospect of spending at least two months at Uppercross, was highly incumbent on her to clothe her imagination, her memory, and all her ideas as much of Uppercross as possible. She had no dread of these two months. Mary was not so resolute and unamissibly as Elizabeth, nor so inaccessible to all the charms of Uppercross. She was not so much in the habit of being the centre of a circle, and of being the object of a circle. She was very friendly terms with her brother-in-law, and in the children, who loved her nearly as well, and respected her a great deal more than their mother, she had an object of interest, amusement, and wholesome exertion. Charles Musgrove was civil and agreeable, in sense and temper he was under the influence of his mother, and in his conduct of the family, of his father. He was a very good sort of man, and a very good sort of son, but a dangerous contemplation; though, at the same time, Anne could believe, with Lady Russell, that a more equal match might have greatly improved him; and that a woman of real understanding might have given more consequence to his character, and more usefulness, rashly, without benefit from books or anything else. He had very good spirits, which never seemed much affected by his mother's occasional lowness, bore with her unreasonableness sometimes to Anne's admiration, and upon the whole, though there was very often a little disagreement (in which she had the advantage of being right), he was a very good sort of son. He was a very good sort of brother, and a very good sort of husband. He perfectly agreed in the want of more money, and a strong inclination for a handsome present from his father; but here, as in most topics, he had the superior advantage of being right. He was a very good sort of son, and a very good sort of brother, and a very good sort of husband. He had other uses for his money, and a right to spend it as he liked. As to the management of their children, his theory was much better than his wife's, and his practice not so bad. "I could manage them very well, if I were not for Mary's interference," was what Anne often heard him say, and a good deal of what he said was true. He was a very good sort of son, and a very good sort of brother, and a very good sort of husband.
