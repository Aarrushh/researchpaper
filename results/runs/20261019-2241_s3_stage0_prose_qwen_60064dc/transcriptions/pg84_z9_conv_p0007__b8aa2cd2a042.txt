I read with ardour those works, so full of genius and discrimination, which modern inquirers have written on these subjects. I attended the lectures and cultivated the acquaintance of the men of science of the university, and I found even in M. Krempe a great deal of merit. I was not, however, able to make any progress in the study of the natural philosophy of the university, but not on that account the least valuable. In M. Waldman I found a true friend. His gentlemanliness was never tinged by dogmatism, and his instructions were given with an air of frankness and good nature that banished every idea of pedantry. In a thousand ways he smoothed me the path of knowledge and made the most abstruse inquiries clear and facile to my apprehension. My application was at first fluctuating and uncertain; it gained strength as progress was made. I was not yet a year old, and I had not yet been in the laboratory. I was still a child, and whilst I was yet engaged in my laboratory, As I applied so closely, it may be easily conceived that my progress was rapid. My ardour was indeed the astonishment of the students, and my proficiency of the masters. Professor Krempe often asked me, with a smile, how Cornelius Agrippa went on, whilst M. Waldman expressed the most heartfelt exultation in my progress. Two years passed in this manner, during which no valid reason was given for my progress, and no reason was given for my decline. None but those who have experienced them can conceive of the enticements of science. In other studies you go as far as all others have gone before you, and there is nothing more to know; but in a scientific pursuit there is continual food for discovery and wonder. A mind of moderate capacity which closely pursues one subject may infallibly become a slave to it, and the more it is pursued, the more it is enslaved. I had become a slave of one object of pursuit and was solely wrapped up in this, improved so rapidly that at the end of two years I made discoveries in the improvement of some chemical instruments, which procured me great esteem and admiration at the university. When I had arrived at this point and had become as well acquainted with the theory and practice of natural philosophy as depended on the lessons of all the professors at Ingolstadt, my residence in the university was terminated, and I returned to my native town, when an incident happened that protracted my stay. One of the phenomena which had peculiarly attracted my attention was the structure of the human frame, and, indeed, any animal ended with life. Whence, I often asked myself, did the principle of life proceed? It was a bold question, and one which has ever been considered as a mystery; yet with many things are we upon the brink of becoming acquainted, if only we could but penetrate the veil of the unknown. I had become acquainted with the structure of the human frame, and determined therethence to apply myself more particularly to those branches of natural philosophy which relate to physiology. Unless I had been animated by an almost supernatural enthusiasm, my application to this study would have been rksome and almost intolerable. To examine the causes of life, we must first have recourse to death. I became acquainted with the science of anatomy, but this was not sufficient; I must observe the natural history of animals, and, finally, anatomy itself.
