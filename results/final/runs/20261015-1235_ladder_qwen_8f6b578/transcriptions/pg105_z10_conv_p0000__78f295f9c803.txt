SIR Walter Elliot, of Kell Lynch Hall, in Somersetshire, was a man who, for his own amusement, never took up any book but the Baronetage; there he found occupation for an idle hour, and consolation in a distressed one; there his faculties were roused under admiration, and respect, by contemplating the remnant of the earliest patents; there any unwelcome sensations, arising from domestic affairs changed naturally into pity and contempt as he turned over the almost endless creations of the last century, and there, if every leaf were powerless, he could read his own history with an interest which never failed. This was the page at which the favourite volume always opened. "ELLIOT OF KELL LYNCH HALL" was the first volume of the Baronetage of James Stevenson, Esq. of South Park, in the county of Gloucester, by which a lady (who died 1800) has its issue Elizabeth, born June 1, 1785; Ann, born August 9, 1787, a still-born son, November 5, 1789; Mary, born November 20, 1791. "Precisely such had the paragraph originally stood from the printer's hands; but Sir Walter had improved it by adding, for the information of himself and his family, these words, after the date of Mary's birth "Married, December 16, 1816, Charles, son and heir of Charles Musgrove, Esq. of Uppercross, the county of Somerset," and by inserting most accurately the day of the month on which he had lost his wife. Then followed the history and rise of the ancient and respectable family, in the usual terms; how it had been first settled in Cheshire; how mentioned in Dugdale, serving the office of high sheriff, representing a borough in three successive parliaments, exertions of loyalty, and dignity of baronet, the first year of Charles II, with all the Marys and Elizabeths they had married, forming altogether two handsome duodecimo pages, and concluding with the arms and motto. "Principal seat, Kell Lynch Hall, in the county of Somerset," and Sir Walter's handwriting again in this finale. "Her presumptive, William Walter Elliot, Esq., great grandson of the second Sir Walter." "Yankee" was the beginning and the end of Sir Walter Elliot's character, vanity of person, and vanity of place. He was a man of fifty-four, still a very fine man. Few women could think more of their personal appearance than he could; nor could the valet of any new made lord be more delighted with the place he held in society. He considered the blessing of beauty as inferior only to the blessing of baronetcy; and the Sir Walter Elliot, who united these gifts, was the constant object of his warmest respect and devotion. His good looks and his rank had one fair claim on his attachment; since to them he must have owed a wife of a very superior character to any thing desired by his own. Lady Elliot had been an excellent woman, sensible and amiable; whose judgement and conduct, if they might be pardoned the unholy infatuation which made Lady Elliot, had never required indulgence afterwards.
