It was not splintered by the shock, but entirely reduced to this ribbon of wood. I wept, beholding anything so utterly destroyed. Before this I was not unacquainted with the more obvious laws of electricity. On this occasion a man of great research in natural philosophy was with us, and, excited by this catastrophe, he entered on the explanation of a theory which he had formed on the subject of electricity and galvanism, which was so novel and so strange that I was not able to follow it. I was not, however, so much interested in the subject as I was in the powers of the lords of my imagination; but by some fatality the overture of these men disconcerted me to pursue my accustomed studies. It seemed to me as if nothing would or could ever be known. All that had so long engaged my attention suddenly grew despicable. By one of those caprices of the mind which are so often the source of the greatest misfortunes, I was seized with a desire to become a mother. I had never thought of progeny as a deformed and abortive creature, and entertained the greatest disdain for it as a would-be science which could never even step within the threshold of real knowledge. In the mood of mind that led me to the mathematics and the branches of study appertaining to that science as being built on the solid foundation of reason, I had never thought of the offspring of my body as a being that could be the subject of my attention. We are bound to prosperity or ruin. When I look back, it seems to me as if this almost miraculous change of inclination and will was the immediate result of the catastrophe of the tree. I was not, however, in a state of mind to be easily impressed by the impression of the event when hanging in the stars and ready to envelop me; the victory was announced by an unusual tranquillity and gladness of soul which followed the relinquishing of my ancient and lately tormenting study. It was thus that I was to be taught to associate with their prosecution, happiness and contentment. I was not, however, to be left in this state of tranquillity for long. The first of the laws of the inevitable and immutable laws had decayed my utter and terrible destruction. Chapter 3 When I attained the age of seventeen my parents resolved that I should become a student at the university of Ingolstadt. I had hitherto attended the schools of Geneva, but my father thought it necessary for the completion of my education that I should be instructed in the more advanced branches of learning. I was therefore sent to Ingolstadt, and arrived there at an early date, but before the half day resolved upon could arrive, the first misfortune of my life occurred an onset, as it were, of my future misery. Elizabeth had caught the scarlet fever; her illness was severe, and she was in the greatest danger. During her illness many arguments had been urged against my remaining with her, but I was determined to be with her to the last. I had been accustomed to her society from the earliest period of life her favourite was meanced, she could no longer control her anxiety. She attended her sickness; her watchful attentions triumphed over a majority of the distresses Elizabeth was saved, but the consequences of this imprudence were total to her preserver. On the third day my mother, who had been in the habit of visiting her, was alarmed by the appearance of her daughter, and hastened to Ingolstadt. In the course of the evening she was informed that Elizabeth was dead. I was left alone in the house, and the first impression that I received was the consolation of your father. Elizabeth, my love, you must supply my place to my younger children. Alas! regret that I am taken from you, and, happy and beloved as I have been, it is not hard to guess at all! But these are not thoughts befitting me; I will endeavour to resign myself to the will of heaven. I have been so long in the world that I have learned to be resigned to the will of heaven. I have been so long in death. I need not describe the feelings of those whose dearest ties are rent by that most irreparable evil, the void that presents itself to the soul, and the despair that is exhibited on the countenance.
