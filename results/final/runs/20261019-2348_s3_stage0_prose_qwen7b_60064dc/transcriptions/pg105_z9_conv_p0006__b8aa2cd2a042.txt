Mr. and Mrs. Musgrove were a very good sort of people, friendly and hospitable, not much educated, and not all at elegant. Their children had more modern minds and manners. There was a numerous family; but the only two grown up, excepting Charles, were Henrietta and Louisa, young ladies of nineteen and twenty, who had brought from school at Exeter all the usual stock of accomplishments, and were now like thousands of other young ladies, living to be fashionable, happy, and merry. Their dress had every advantage, their faces were rather pretty, their manners good, their manner unembarrassed and pleasant, and they were conversant with the world, and were therefore always ready to be considered as some of the happiest creatures of her acquaintance; but still, saved as we are, by some comfortable feeling of superiority from wishing for the possibility of exchange, she would not have given up her own elegant and cultivated mind for all their enjoyments, and envied them nothing but that seemingly perfect good understanding and agreeable together, that good-humoured mutual affection, of which she could know so little herself, and which she could not have had, had she been in their situation. Nothing could have entered among the side of the Great House family, which was generally, as Anne very well knew, the least to blame. The half hour was chatted away pleasantly enough; and she was not at all surprised, at the end of it, to have their walking party joined by both the Miss Musgroves, at Mary's particular invitation. CHAPTER VI. Anne had not wanted this visit to Uppercross, to learn to remember the new set of people, and to acquire the acquaintance of three miles, will often offer her; but she had wanted to see them, and she had. Never had she been staying there before, without being struck by, or without wishing that other Elliots could have their advantage in seeing how unknown, or undiscovered there, were the affairs which at Kellney Hall were treated as of such general publicity and pervading interest; yet, with all this experience, she believed she must now submit to feel that another visit was necessary. She had been there, she knew, for two years, and she had been there for two years; for certainly, coming as she did, with a heart full of the subject which had been completely occupying both houses in Kellney for many weeks, she had expected rather more curiosity and sympathy than she found there in the separate but very similar remark of Mr. and Mrs. Musgrove. "So, Miss Anne, Sir Walter and your sister are gone; and what part of Bath do you think they will settle in?" and this, without much waiting for an answer; or in the young ladies, "We have been so much in Bath, and so much in Bath, and so much in Bath, that we must be in a good situation: none of your Queen Squares for us!" or in the annexes supplement from Mary, "Of—Upon my word, I shall be pretty well off, when you are all gone away to be happy at Bath!" She could only resolve to avoid such self-deception in future, and with thoughtless gratitude of the extraordinary blessing of having one such truly sympathetic friend as Lady Russell. The Miss Musgroves had their own gaiety to guard, and to destroy, their own gaiety, blogs, and schemes to engage in, and their own gaiety, blogs, and schemes to call to all their common subjects of housekeeping, neighbours, dress, dancing, and music. She acknowledged it would be very fitting, that every little social commonwealth should dictate its own matters of discourse; and hoped, ere long, to become a not unworthy member of the one she was now transplanted into. With the prospect of spending at least two months at Uppercross, it was highly incumbent on her to clothe her imagination, her memory, and all her feelings with the most of Uppercross as possible, and to make the best of them.
