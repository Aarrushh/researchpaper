"Now, Doctor, but none the less you must come round to my view, for otherwise I shall keep on piling fact upon fact upon you until your reason breaks down under them and acknowledges me to be right. Now, Mr. Jabez Wilson here has been good enough to call upon me this morning, and to begin a narrative which promises to be one of the most singular which I have listened to for some time. You have heard me remark that the strangest and most unique things are very often connected not with the larger but with the smaller crimes, and occasionally, indeed, where there is room for doubt whether any particular crime has been committed. As far as I have heard, it is impossible for me to say whether Mr. Wilson's case is a strange one or not. It is certainly one of the most singular and certainly one of the most singular that I have ever listened to. Perhaps, Mr. Wilson, you would have the kindness to recommence your narrative. I ask you not merely because my friend Dr. Watson has not heard the opening part but also because the peculiar nature of the story makes me anxious to have every possible detail from your lips. As a rule, when I have heard some slight indication of the course of events, I am able to guide myself by the thought that the story will be a strange one, and I am therefore able to make allowances. But I cannot admit that the facts are, to the best of my belief, unique." The portly client puffed out his chest with an appearance of some little pride and pulled a dirty and wrinkled newspaper from the inside pocket of his greatcoat. As he glanced down the advertisement column, with his head thrust forward and the paper flattened out upon his knee, I took a good look at the man. He was, after all, a man of some importance, judging from the advertisements which might be read from his dress or appearance. I did not gain very much, however, by my inspection. Our visitor bore every mark of being an average commonplace British tradesman, obese, pompous, and slow. He wore rather baggy grey sheep's check trousers, a not-over-clean black frock-coat, unbuttoned in front, and a drab waistcoat with a heavy brass Albert chain, and a square pierced bit of metal dangling down as an ornament. He had a pair of thick-rimmed spectacles, which he had just been adjusting, hanging loosely round his neck, and a pair of leather gloves, which he had just been pulling off, lying beside him. Altogether, look as I would, there was nothing remarkable about the man save his blazing red head, and the expression of extreme chargin and discontent upon his features. Sherlock Holmes' quick eye took in his appearance, and he shook his head with a smile as he noticed my questioning glances. "Beyond the obvious facts that he has at some time done manual labour, that he takes snuff, that he is a Freemason, that he has been in China, and that he has been in the East, I have nothing to go upon. Now, Mr. Wilson, what do you say?" Mr. Wilson started up in his chair, with his forefinger upon the paper, but his eyes upon my companion. "How, in the name of good-fortune, did you know all that, Mr. Holmes?" he asked. "How did you know, for example, that I did manual labour. It is as true as gospel, for I began as a ship's carpenter." "My heavens, my dear sir. Your right hand is a rather larger than your left. You have worked with it, I think, the fingers are more developed."
