Anne walked up at the same time, in a sort of despondent tranquility, to the Lodge, where she was to spend the first week. Her friend was not in better spirits than herself. Lady Russell felt this break-up of the family exceedingly. Their respectability was as dear to her as her own, and a daily intercourse had become precious by habit. It was painful to look upon their deserted grounds, and still worse to anticipate the new hands they were to fall to; and to escape the solitariness and the melancholy of so altered a village, and be out of the way when Admiral and Mrs Croft first arrived, she had determined to make her own absence from home begin when she went up. Accordingly their removal was made together, and Anne was set down at Uppercross Cottage, a few miles from the Great House, and a few miles from the village. The cottage was a small one, a few years back had been completely in the old English style, containing only two hours superior in appearance to those of the yeomen and labourers; the mansion of the square, with its high walls, great gates, and old trees, substantial and unmoderred, and the compact, tight parsonage, enclosed in its own neat garden, with a vine and a pear-tree standing round its casements; but upon the marriage of the young 'squire', it had received the improvements of family taste and wealth. The house was a large one, with a great hall, a great dining-room, with its veranda, French windows, and other prettiness, was quite as likely to catch the traveller's eye as the more consistent and considerable aspect and premises of the Great House, about a quarter of a mile farther on. Here Anne had often been staying. She knew the ways of Uppercross as well as those of Kellincy. The two families, were, of course, connected; and she had been there, as she had been at the Great House, a host of times. It was not a surprise for her to find Mary alone; being alone, her being unwell and of spirits was almost a matter of course. Though better than ever did the elder sister, Mary had not Anne's understanding nor temper. While well, and happy, and properly attended to, she had great good humour and excellent spirits; but any indisposition sunk her completely. She had no resources for solitude, and inventing a considerable share of the Elliot family's faults, she was always ready to find fault with the rest of the family, and to be ill and ill-used. In person, she was inferior to both sisters, and had, even in her bloom, only reached the dignity of being "a fine girl." She was now lying on the faded sofa of the pretty little drawing-room, upon one elegant furniture of which had been gradually growing shabby, under the influence of four summers and two children; and, on Anne's appearing, she greeted her with "So, you are come at last! I begin to think I should never have you, I am so ill I can scarce speak." Having said this, she added, "I am glad to see you, and I am glad to replace Anne. 'You sent me such a good account of yourself on Thursday.'" "Yes, I made the best of it; I always do; but was very far from all the time; and I do not think I ever was so ill in my life as have been all this morning: very unfit to be left alone, I am sure. Suppose I were to be seized of a sudden in some dreadful way, and not able to ring the bell: So, so, I should be in a fright out. I do not know how she has got through these last times this summer, and what was proper, and what was not, after her husband. 'Oh! Chawton is a shocking place; I have not seen him since seven o'clock. He would go, though I told him how ill I was. He said he should not stay out long; but he has never come back, and now it is almost one. I assure you, I have not seen a soul this whole long morning." "You have had your little boys with you?" "Yes, as long as I could bear their noises; but they are so unmanageable, and do me more harm than good. Mr Harlel does not mind a word I say, but Walter is growing quite as bad. I wish you would come to me better now," "I can give you no account of them. I have not always cure you when I come. How are your neighbours at the Great House?"
