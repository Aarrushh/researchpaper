It was not splintered by the shock, but entirely reduced to its millions of wood, I never beheld anything so utterly destroyed. Before this I was not unacquainted with the more obvious laws of electricity. On this occasion a man of great research in natural philosophy was with us, and excited by this catastrophe, he entered on the explanation of a theory which he had formed on the subject of electricity and galvanism, which was at once new and astonishing to me. All that he said threw greatly into the shade Corotus Agrippa, Albertus Magnus, and Paracelsus: the lords of my imagination; but by some fatality the overthrow of the tree was not to be explained by any of the theories which he mentioned. It seemed as if nothing would or could ever be known. All that had so long engaged my attention suddenly grew despicable. By one of those caprices of the mind which we are perhaps most subject to in early youth, I at once gave up my former occupations, set down natural history and all its progeny as a deformed and abortive creation, and entertained the greatest desire for a would-be science which could never even step within the threshold of real knowledge. In the mind of man, the desire for knowledge is the first and most important of all. It is the foundation of that science as being built upon secure foundations, and so worthy of my consideration. Thus strangely are our souls constructed, and by such slight ligaments are we bound to prosperity or ruin. When I look back, it seems to me as if this almost miraculous change of inclination and will was the immediate suggestion of the guardian angel of my life, who made me the slave of the spirit of preservation, and who, by the guidance of the stars and ready to endow me, had been so long beguiled by an unnatural tranquillity and ghastliness of life which followed the relinquishing of my ancient and latterly tormenting studies. It was thus that I was to be taught to associate evil with their prosecution, happiness with their disregard. It was a strong effort of the spirit of good, but it was ineffacable. Destiny was too potent, and her immutable hand had decreed my utter and terrible destruction. I had been a student of the science of nature, and had been persuaded to become a student at the university of Ingolstadt. I had hitherto attended the schools of Geneva, but my father thought it necessary for the completion of my education that I should be made acquainted with other customs than those of my native country. My departure was therefore fixed at an early date, but before the day resolved upon could arrive, the first misfortune of my life occurred—an omen, as it were, of my future misery. Elizabeth had caught the scarlet fever, and died of it. I had been left in the hands of a woman who had been recommended to me, and had been urged to persuade my mother to refrain from attending upon her. She had at first yielded to our entreaties, but when she heard that the life of her favourite was menaced, she could no longer control her anxiety. She attended her sickness; her watchful attentions triumphed over the malignity of the distemper—Elizabeth was saved, but the consequences of this imprudence were fatal to her presence. On the third day my mother's sickness became worse, and was accompanied by a most singular symptom, and the look of her attendants prognosticated the worst event. On her deathbed the fortitude and benignity of this best of women did not desert her. She joined the hands of Elizabeth and myself. "My children," she said, "my firmest hopes of future happiness were placed on the prospect of your union. This expectation will now be the consolation of your father, and, believe me, you, will most surely place my to my younger children. Allas! I regret that I am taken from you; and happy and blessed will you be, if you can make your life and mine a blessing to each other.
