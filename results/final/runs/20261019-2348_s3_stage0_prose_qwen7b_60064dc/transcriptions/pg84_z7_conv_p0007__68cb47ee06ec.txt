Chapter 4 From this day natural philosophy, and particularly chemistry, in the most comprehensive sense of the term, became nearly my sole occupation. I read with those works, so full of genius and knowledge, that I had been so long desirous of obtaining. I was now in possession of the keys of the most extensive science of the university, and I found in Mr. Kempe a great deal of sound sense and real information, combined, it is true, with a repulsive physiognomy and manners, but not on that account the less valuable. Mr. W. Maufain I found a true friend. His gentleness was never tinged by obsequiousness, and his knowledge was always accompanied by a kind of modesty which I found very agreeable. He was a man of great sense, who smoothed me on my path of knowledge and made the most abstruse inquiries clear and facile to my apprehension. By application was at first fluctuating and uncertain, it gained strength as it progressed and soon became so ardent and eager that the stars often disappeared in the light of my eyes. I was now in the full enjoyment of the most delightful sensations. I was no longer the same man. My former ardour was indeed the indulgence of the students, and my proficiency that of the masters. Professor Kempe often asked me, with a sly smile, why I did not take a degree. I answered that I was not yet ready for it. I was not then in the condition of a young man, a wanderer, during which I paid no visit to Geneva, but was engaged heart and soul, in the pursuit of some discoveries which I hoped to make. None but those who have experienced them can conceive of the enticements of science. In other studies you go as far as others have gone before you, and there is no further progress to be made. But in natural philosophy, the more you know, the more you are aware of the vastness of the field which closely passes us; one study must infinitely怀里 arrive at great proficiency in that study, and I, who continually sought the attainment of one object and was so wisely wrapped up in this, improved so rapidly that at the end of two years I made some discoveries in the improvement of chemistry, which I have since published. I was now in possession of the keys of the university, and I had become so well acquainted with the theory and practice of natural philosophy as depended on the lessons of any of the professors at Ingolstadt, my residence there being no longer conducive to my improvements, thought of returning to my friends and my native town, when an incident happened which changed my plans. I was one day walking in the garden of the house of my friend, Mr. Maufain, a man of science, and, indeed, any animal endued with life. Whence, I often asked myself, did the principle of life proceed? It was a bold question, and one which has ever been considered as a mystery, yet with how many things we are upon the brink of becoming acquainted, if cowardice or carelessness did not prevent us from passing over the threshold.
