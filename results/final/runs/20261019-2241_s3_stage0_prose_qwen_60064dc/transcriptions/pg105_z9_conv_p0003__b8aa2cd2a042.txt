First, Lady Russell walked in at the same time, in a state of desolate tranquillity, to the lodge, where she had to spend the first week. Her friend was now in better spirits than herself. Lady Russell left this building and the family exceedingly. Their resemblance was as dear to her as her own, and a daily intimacy had become precious by habit. It was painful to look upon their deserted ground, and still worse to anticipate the new hands they were to fall into; and to escape the solitariness and the melancholy of so altered a village, and be out of the way when Admiral Mrs Croft first arrived, she had determined to make her own absence from home begin when she must give up Anne. Accordingly their removal was made together, and Anne was seated up at Upperpiece. Catherine, who had been a guest at the house, was now gone, and a few years had been completely spent in the old English style, containing only two houses superior in appearance to those of the yeomen and labourers; the mansion of the squire, with its high walls, great gates, and old trees, substantial and unmodernized, and the compact, tight,paragus, enclosed in its own neat garden, with a vine and a pear-tree trained round its casements; but upon the marriage of the young squire, it had received the improvements of the new style, and was now a large, handsome, and commodious house, with its veranda, French windows, and other prettiness, was quite as likely to catch the traveller's eye as the more consistent and considerable aspect and premises of the Great House, about a quarter of a mile farther on. Here Anne had often been staying. She knew the ways of Upperpiece as well as those of Kellhyth. The two families were so continually meeting, so much in the habit, of running in and out of each other's house at all hours that it was quite a matter of course. But, though ever better than the elder sister, Mary had not Anne's understanding nor temper. While well, and happy, and properly attended to, she had great good humour and excellent spirits; but any indisposition sulked her completely. She had no resources for solitude, and inheriting a considerable share of the Elton fortune, she had no means of spending it. She was a woman of great wit, but of a wit ill-used. In person, she was inferior to both sisters, and had, even in her bloom, only reached the dignity of being a "fine girle." She was now lying on the faded sofa of the little pretty drawing-room, the once elegant furniture of which had been gradually growing shabby, under the influence of four summers and two children; and, on Anne's appearing, greeted with her "So, you are come at last!
